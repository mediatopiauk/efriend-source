17 years of sharpening
Bristol's knives.

A short history of a long apprenticeship. How a former tool-and-die maker in Bridgnorth ended up holding the edges of the south west's best kitchens — and what we've learned along the way about steel, patience, and saying no.

Lewis Whitcombe Founder · Severn Cut

Seventeen years ago this April, I sharpened a knife for someone who didn't know me, in a workshop that didn't have a roof, with a wheel I'd bought off a man who was retiring from sharpening agricultural blades in Telford. The knife belonged to a chef called Idris Owen, who had just opened a restaurant in Clifton and had been told by a fishmonger to "find the man in Bridgnorth who does the proper job." I charged him £4.50. He paid in cash. He came back the following week with eleven more knives and a bottle of single malt. I have not had a quiet Thursday since.

This is the short story of those seventeen years — and a few of the things they've taught us about what we're actually doing when we put a knife to a stone.

§ 01The first knife

I trained as a tool-and-die maker at Caterpillar in Wolverhampton. I left in 2007 because the work had gone — the line where I'd done my apprenticeship was being shipped to Belgium — and because I'd just started a family. I needed something I could do in a shed. The shed I had was my grandfather's, behind the cottage on Hospital Street. The first thing I sharpened in it was my own carving knife. The second thing I sharpened in it was my neighbour Brian's secateurs. The third was Idris Owen's Sabatier.

That Sabatier is on the wall above the bench. The handle is darker than it was. The bolster has been thinned twice. The blade is now about 2.4 mm shorter, top to spine, than it was when Idris brought it in. He gave it to me last year when he sold the restaurant. He said he wanted to know it was being looked after.

§ 02Why we left commission work

For the first nine years we made knives. Not many. A friend in Bristol — Hannah, the chef at Box Hall — wanted a santoku for her own use. Then four more. Then six for her team. Word moved sideways through the south west the way it always does in this trade: one chef tells another chef, who tells the supplier they were having lunch with.

By 2017 we had a six-month backlog of commission orders for chef's knives. We had hired Mei (still here, now runs heat-treatment) and Joe (here for three years, now in Sheffield doing his own thing). We were making twelve knives a month. Each one took thirty-six to fifty hours. Each one sold for between £380 and £950.

And we were not very happy.

Making the knife is the easiest part. Sharpening someone else's knife — properly, every six weeks for ten years — is what actually keeps a kitchen alive. — What Mei said to me, in 2017, after she finally got bored of me being miserable about it

She was right. We took every knife-making order off the books that quarter. We paid back the deposits. We wrote thirty-one apology letters and sent them with bottles of decent wine. We bought a third grinding wheel, a Tormek, and a proper Japanese pull-through stone setup. We have not made a knife to commission since 2017. We sharpen between four and six hundred a month.

§ 03How a kitchen actually wears its knives

If you ask twenty chefs how they think a kitchen wears its knives, you'll get twenty answers — and almost all of them will mention the chopping board. The chopping board is part of it. It's nowhere near the whole of it. Here is what actually happens, in roughly the order it happens:

  • Day one to day four: the factory edge wears off. This is not the same as the knife going blunt. The factory edge is a polished bevel that's optimised for being shiny in a shop window, not for cutting a tomato. The knife will feel less "scary" by day four. It is not blunter; it has just become its true self.
  • Week two to month six: the steel work-hardens at the cutting edge. This is good. A well-treated AUS-10 or VG-10 blade will hold a working edge for ten to fourteen weeks of brigade use without any intervention beyond a honing steel.
  • Month six onwards: the bevel rolls. Microscopic deformations on the edge add up. The knife feels "okay" but lazy. This is when most chefs phone us. They are almost always six weeks too late.
  • Year one to year three: if the knife has been honed properly, the only thing that needs doing is a stone re-profile — about forty minutes' work, £18 per knife.
  • Year three to year ten: the bolster needs thinning. The handle may need replacing (especially Pakka and resin handles in dishwasher kitchens). The choil may need a slight re-grind.

Most of the work we do is on knives that are between four and nine years old, owned by chefs who treat them well, and who would rather pay us £24 every six weeks than spend £400 every two years on a replacement.

§ 04The Bristol chefs who turned up

The reason there's a thirty-eight-mile loop on our van's dashcam every Tuesday — Bridgnorth, Worcester, Cheltenham, Bath, Bristol, back — is that Bristol has, in the last decade, become the best food city in England that isn't London. I'm biased. So is everyone we collect from. Here's a partial list of who we collect from on Tuesdays:

  • Box Hall — Hannah's team. Twelve knives, every six weeks.
  • Forge & Crown — eight knives, every five weeks. (Their cleaver lives a hard life.)
  • Pasta Loco — six knives.
  • Casamia — confidential.
  • The Ethicurean — five knives, plus a wheel-sharpened set of butchery tools every quarter.
  • Bulrush, Bell's Diner, Marmo, Source, Wilson's, Birch — between four and ten knives each.

We also sharpen for around forty home cooks who've found us through one of those chefs. We do not advertise. We have a website because Mei made me make one in 2019. Half the photos on it are wrong. I'll get round to it.

§ 05What we'd tell a 26-year-old us

If I could write a postcard to the version of me who was bent over Idris Owen's Sabatier in April 2009, this is what it would say:

  1. Buy the microscope earlier.
  2. Charge enough that you can afford to do the job properly. The chefs you want will pay it. The ones who won't are not your customers.
  3. When you say "no" to a commission order, you create the conditions to say "yes" to a chef who needs a knife sharpened the day before a wedding. That second yes is the better business.
  4. Hire Mei in 2014, not 2016.
  5. The roof. Just do the roof.

§ 06What's next

In 2026 we're doing three things differently. First, we're opening on Saturdays — by appointment, two slots a week, for chefs who can only escape on weekends. Second, we're teaching. Four Saturday workshops a year, four students each, £180 a head. You leave with a knife you've sharpened on a wheel, on a Tormek, and on three different stones — and an understanding of why your kitchen-drawer knife has felt wrong since 2018. Third, we're hiring. Slowly. A third pair of hands, ideally a former toolmaker, ideally someone who's tired of cities.

Other than that — Tuesdays in Bristol, Wednesdays at the wheel, the rest of the week answering emails, and the roof, finally, holding up.

— Lewis, May 2026.
